# The Fluency Contract

This page says what the course asks of you and what it does not. Read
it before Chapter 1. Your instructor will point back to it all term.

<!-- --8<-- [start:contract] -->
**The aim.** This course prepares you to be the human in the loop: the person who directs an AI tool, checks its work, and decides when it should not be used at all. The district calls that AI fluency. Most courses that use the phrase stop at recognizing AI and trying a chat tool. This one does not.

**Three bars, and the one this course clears.** A typical AI literacy course asks you to know what AI is. An AI engineering course asks you to build it. This course sits between them. You will not write code, do math, or train a model. You will produce work a manager could act on, and you will be able to defend every claim in it.

| A literacy course asks you to | This course asks you to | An engineering course asks you to (not this course) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recognize AI when you see it | Classify an AI system by where its behavior comes from, and say which problems that type fits | Build and train models |
| Try a chat tool | Design, test, and refine prompts against written criteria, with a log a colleague can audit | Program against model APIs and build agents |
| Know that AI can be wrong | Verify AI output with a method, audit it for accuracy and bias, and report what you found | Compute model metrics and tune models |
| Discuss ethics | Apply named frameworks and laws to a real decision and write the policy | Engineer privacy and fairness into a system |
| Hear about tools | Compare platforms with stated criteria, select one for a specific problem, and measure a pilot | Integrate and deploy AI in production |
| Write a reflection | Deliver a project with a verification trail and present it to a stakeholder | Ship a system |

**Five habits of the human in the loop.** Every chapter practices at least one. Every Skills Lab is graded on them.

1. Keep a log. Every prompt, every response, every check, copied exactly.
2. Verify before you trust. Fluent is not the same as true.
3. Protect what is not yours to share. Nothing goes into a tool that you would not post in public.
4. Choose by criteria. The tool fits the problem, not the other way around.
5. Know when to say no. Some tasks should not go to an AI tool, and you can say why.

**What comes next.** CIS144 builds agents and runs AI-driven analytics. CIS218 integrates and deploys AI in applications. This course points at both without teaching them.

## How the contract reaches your grade

Every Skills Lab has three parts. Part 3 always produces something a manager could act on: a memo, a comparison matrix, a policy, a pilot plan. The two Questions & Analysis items ask you to cite your own log as evidence. The [Skills Lab Rubric](skills-lab-rubric.md) grades the result. Its Mastery level describes work a colleague could build on without asking you a question. That level is the human in the loop.

## How the contract reads the course competencies

The six official competencies stay exactly as the district wrote them (see the home page). This course reads each one at the level IT professionals expect of a bachelor's student:

* Evaluate responses for accuracy: with a repeatable method and a written finding, not a hunch.
* Differentiate the types of AI: well enough to say which type a business problem needs.
* Demonstrate prompting strategies: with criteria, versions, and a log.
* Analyze the state of AI and its impact: from sourced evidence, including the impact on work.
* Apply ethical guidelines: by name, to a decision, in a policy someone else can follow.
* Select AI solutions: with criteria, a pilot, and a recommendation you can defend.

## Who this course is for

Phoenix College offers CIS137 as an elective in its bachelor's pathway in information technology, and recommends it as a first choice. The course has no prerequisite, so every chapter teaches from zero. The bar is set by what you produce, not by what you already know.
