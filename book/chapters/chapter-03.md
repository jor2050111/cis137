## 3.1 AI as a Research Partner

A chat assistant is a fast research partner and a poor final source. It can summarize a document, brainstorm angles you missed, and point you toward places that might hold an answer. It cannot promise that those places exist, or that they say what it claims. Chapter 2 explained why: the model predicts likely text, and a likely-sounding source is not a real one.

So treat what it gives you as a **lead**: a pointer to where an answer might be located, not the answer itself. A lead says "the county health department probably publishes heat data." A fact says "the county reported this number in this report." You turn a lead into a fact by opening the source yourself.

The source you want is a **primary source**, the organization or document where a fact starts: the agency that collected the data, the study that ran the test, the text of the rule. A news story about a county report is useful, but the report is the primary source. When you write the fact down, add a **citation**, a reference that names the source well enough for someone else to find it. A citation gives the publisher, the title, the date, and the location.

### Three Tools, Three Jobs

You have three kinds of tools for research, and each one fits a different moment.

| Tool | What it gives you | What you still have to do | When it fits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Search engine | A list of pages that match your words | Pick the pages, judge who wrote them, read them | You know what you are looking for and need the page itself |
| Chat assistant | A written answer with no guaranteed source | Treat every claim as a lead and find its source | You need ideas, angles, search terms, or a summary of text you supply |
| Research tool | A written answer with citations you can open | Open each citation and confirm it says what the answer claims | You need a first pass at a question with sources attached |

A research tool looks like the safe choice, and it is often the best starting point, although its citations still need checking. A research tool can cite a real page that does not support the sentence it is attached to, so the check is to open the page and find the sentence it supports.

### Asking for Leads, Not Answers

Here is a research prompt from a neighborhood nonprofit. The nonprofit, in Phoenix, is writing a handout on summer heat and outdoor workers, and a volunteer is doing the research. Notice what the prompt asks for and what it forbids. Send the prompt without the [Step] lines. They show you the decision behind each part.

```text
[Step 1: say who the research is for and what it will become]
A neighborhood nonprofit in Phoenix wants to write a one-page handout on how summer heat affects people who work outdoors. I am doing the research.
[Step 2: ask for leads you can check, not for answers]
Give me five leads I can check: for each, name the kind of organization likely to publish reliable information and what I should search for on its site.
[Step 3: forbid the thing you could not trust anyway]
Do not give me any statistics.
[Step 4: set a shape you can work through one line at a time]
One line per lead.
```

Sample response (captured from a chat assistant, October 2026). Your output will differ:

```text
1. **Federal workplace safety agency (OSHA):** search its site for "heat illness prevention," "Heat Hazard Recognition," and its employer and worker guidance on water, rest, shade, and acclimatization.
2. **Federal occupational health research institute (NIOSH/CDC):** search for "Criteria for a Recommended Standard: Occupational Exposure to Heat and Hot Environments" and "heat stress" to find symptoms, first aid, and acclimatization schedules.
3. **National Weather Service / federal heat-health portal (Heat.gov, NWS Phoenix):** search for "HeatRisk," "Extreme Heat Warning," and "heat safety for outdoor workers" to explain how forecasts and alerts are issued and what they mean.
4. **Local public health department (Maricopa County Department of Public Health):** search for "heat-associated deaths report," "heat relief network," and "heat illness symptoms" to find local findings, cooling center locations, and hydration advice.
5. **State and city government offices (Arizona Division of Occupational Safety and Health, City of Phoenix Office of Heat Response and Mitigation):** search for "heat rule," "heat standard," and "heat safety resources" to confirm what current requirements and local programs apply to Phoenix workers.
```

Five leads, no numbers, one line each: the prompt did its job, and now the leads have to earn their place. Here is what checking two of them looked like on October 21, 2026:

* **Lead 4 held.** Maricopa County Public Health's site has a page titled "Heat Reports" that lists a yearly heat-related deaths report for each year, with the 2025 report the newest. The lead named the right publisher and a close search term.
* **Lead 3 held.** The National Weather Service runs a Phoenix office page at weather.gov, and the weather service publishes a HeatRisk forecast map that rates each day's heat danger.

Lead 5 needs a closer look before you trust it. It tells you to search for a "heat rule" and a "heat standard" to confirm what requirements apply. Whether Arizona has either is a fact the lead does not give you. Search terms can carry hidden claims like that one.

Record each check the same way: the lead, the page you opened, its publisher, its date, and whether it held. That record is your research brief, and Skills Lab 3A asks you for one.

### Summarizing a Source You Supply

Summaries are the safest research assignment you can give a chat assistant, because you supply the information. The prompt below hands over a charter school's visitor email and asks for two things: a summary, and a list of what the email leaves out.

```text
Summarize the email below in three bullet points for the teachers at a charter school. Use only the text in the email. After the bullets, list two questions a teacher might ask that the email does not answer.

Email: Beginning Monday, March 2, every visitor must sign in at the front office tablet and wear a printed badge. Parents picking up a student early must show a photo ID the first time. Substitute teachers will receive a badge from the office manager each morning. The tablet will be out of service during the first week of spring break for a software update.
```

Sample response (captured from a chat assistant, October 2026). Your output will differ:

```text
- Starting Monday, March 2, all visitors must sign in on the front office tablet and wear a printed badge.
- Parents picking up a student early must show a photo ID the first time they do so.
- Substitute teachers will get a badge from the office manager each morning.

One more detail: the sign-in tablet will be out of service during the first week of spring break for a software update.

**Questions the email does not answer:**
1. What should visitors do to sign in while the tablet is out of service during spring break?
2. Do staff, volunteers, or regular contractors who aren't visitors or substitutes also need a badge or need to sign in?
```

Run the three passes from Chapter 1. Each line traces to a sentence in the email, so nothing was invented, and the gap list is the most valuable part. It found a hole the office manager should fill before sending: how visitors sign in while the tablet is down. Notice the shape, though. The prompt asked for three bullets, and the tool kept the count by moving the fourth fact into a line below them.

### Fix It 3.1: The Summary That Read Well 🔧

A volunteer at a neighborhood food bank wanted a quick summary of the new sign-up form's first month to read aloud at the volunteer meeting.

**Symptom:** The prompt asked for a short summary:

```text
Summarize this update in two short sentences, under 30 words total, for the volunteers at a neighborhood food bank.

Update: Our new online sign-up form went live on September 8. In its first month, 214 families registered, up from 160 the month before with paper forms. Average wait time at the Saturday pickup dropped from 40 minutes to 25 minutes. Volunteers at the check-in table now scan a code instead of looking up names in a binder. The form is only in English for now, so families who read Spanish or Vietnamese still need help at the table, and we expect their numbers to stay undercounted until the translations arrive. Two volunteers built the form with a free tool. We will add a reminder text feature in November. Thank you to everyone who tested it.
```

Here is the response it gave (captured from a chat assistant, October 2026):

```text
Our new online sign-up has registered 214 families in its first month and cut Saturday pickup waits from 40 to 25 minutes. It's English-only for now, so Spanish and Vietnamese readers still need table help; reminder texts arrive in November.
```

It reads well, every fact in it came from the update, and it ran 40 words against a 30-word limit. Compare it with the update line by line before you read on.

**Diagnose:** Before you touch the prompt, name in one sentence what a volunteer would now believe that the update did not say, and whether that matters more than the missed word limit.

**Repair:** The update said the registrations from Spanish and Vietnamese readers stay undercounted until translations arrive. The summary kept the number and dropped the warning that came with it, so 214 now reads as a full count. The prompt never said which fact had to survive, so the model chose. Name the caveat, and tell the model what to give up instead:

```text
Summarize this update in two short sentences, under 30 words total, for the volunteers at a neighborhood food bank. The summary must keep the caveat that the registration count is undercounted because the form is only in English. If you cannot fit every number, drop a number, never the caveat.

Update: Our new online sign-up form went live on September 8. In its first month, 214 families registered, up from 160 the month before with paper forms. Average wait time at the Saturday pickup dropped from 40 minutes to 25 minutes. Volunteers at the check-in table now scan a code instead of looking up names in a binder. The form is only in English for now, so families who read Spanish or Vietnamese still need help at the table, and we expect their numbers to stay undercounted until the translations arrive. Two volunteers built the form with a free tool. We will add a reminder text feature in November. Thank you to everyone who tested it.
```

Sample response (captured from a chat assistant, October 2026). Your output will differ:

```text
The new online sign-up cut Saturday waits from 40 to 25 minutes. Registrations (214) are undercounted because the form is English-only, so Spanish and Vietnamese speakers still need table help.
```

What changed: one thing. The prompt now names the fact that must survive and the rule for what to cut, and the 30-word limit stays where it was.

**Verify:** Find the caveat in the new summary: "Registrations (214) are undercounted because the form is English-only." It is there, attached to the number it qualifies. Because the limit did not move, the caveat rule is what brought it back. To make room, the tool dropped the launch date and the paper-form figure, the trade the rule asked for. Now count the words: 30, and the limit was under 30, so it still misses by one. The repair fixed the failure that mattered and missed the smaller one, so you still count. The limit is not the whole cause, either. Sent with no word limit at all, the same update also lost the undercount. Two other notices with caveats, a bus pass program and a text-reminder trial, kept theirs.

## 3.2 Creative Work with AI

Generative AI produces text, images, audio, and video. Creative work is where it saves the most time and where its failures are easiest to miss, because a polished draft invites you to stop reading. The skill in this section is the one you used on research. Supply the facts and set the limits. Then check what comes back against both.

### Text

Text is the strongest creative use, because a chat assistant gets you past a blank page, offers versions you can react to, and fits a draft to a length or a reader. It also fails in predictable ways. It adds details nobody gave it and drifts from the requested length, while its voice slides toward a polished sameness that readers skim.

Here is a request from a volunteer at a neighborhood nonprofit that runs a free bike repair clinic. Send the prompt without the [Step] lines:

```text
[Step 1: say who is asking and what the event is]
I volunteer for a neighborhood nonprofit that runs a free bike repair clinic in a church parking lot.
[Step 2: name the job, the length, and the reader]
Write a 120-word blurb for the community events page. Audience: neighbors who ride to work or school and cannot afford a repair shop.
[Step 3: hand over every fact, and forbid the rest]
Facts to use: every Saturday, 8 a.m. to 11 a.m.; volunteers fix flats, brakes, and chains; parts are free while supplies last; no appointment needed. Do not invent any other facts, names, or numbers.
[Step 4: set the voice]
Plain, friendly tone, no exclamation points.
```

Sample response (captured from a chat assistant, October 2026). Your output will differ:

```text
**Free Bike Repair Clinic, Every Saturday**

If your bike gets you to work or school, but a repair shop isn't in the budget, we can help. Every Saturday from 8 a.m. to 11 a.m., our volunteers run a free bike repair clinic in the church parking lot.

Bring your bike and we will fix flats, brakes, and chains. Parts are free while supplies last. You don't need an appointment, so just roll in whenever it suits you during clinic hours.

Our neighborhood nonprofit believes everyone deserves a reliable ride. Stop by, say hello, and get back on the road with a bike that works the way it should.
```

Run the three passes, starting with the first. Did it do what was asked? Partly. All four facts are there, with no exclamation points, but a word counter gives 102 words for the body, well short of the 120 requested. The tool never mentioned the length, so only your own count catches it. Is each claim true? Every fact matches the prompt, and no new names or numbers appear. What did it add? A line saying the nonprofit "believes everyone deserves a reliable ride," plus "Stop by, say hello." Neither is a fact, so neither breaks the rule, but the first one speaks for the nonprofit. The volunteer still decides whether the nonprofit wants that line under its name.

### Images

Chapter 1 introduced the image generator, which turns a text prompt into a picture. This book cannot show you a captured image the way it shows captured text, so here is a prompt the bike clinic volunteer could write, and the checks to run on whatever comes back:

```text
A flat illustration of two volunteers fixing a bicycle in a sunny church parking lot, warm desert colors, no text, no logos, no recognizable faces.
```

Check every generated image for these problems before you use it:

* **Text in the picture.** Generators often produce letters that look like words and are not. "No text" in the prompt is a request, not a guarantee.
* **People who look real.** A face that resembles a real person is a risk you do not need. Prefer illustration styles and no recognizable faces.
* **Logos and brands.** A shape that looks like a company's logo can imply a sponsor you do not have.
* **Accuracy to the event.** Count the bikes, the tools, and the people. An image of a bike shop is not a parking-lot clinic.
* **Alt text.** Write a one-sentence description of what the image shows, so a reader using a screen reader gets the same message. The generator does not write it for you.

### Audio and Video

Generative tools also produce speech, music, and video. They can narrate a slideshow, translate a short video's audio, or read a document aloud for a listener who cannot see it. The same tools can imitate a person. A **deepfake** is synthetic audio, video, or an image that makes a real person appear to say or do something they never did. What makes a deepfake harmful is a real person shown doing what they never did, without their consent and without telling the audience. Section 3.4 takes up consent and honesty.

| Medium | Where it helps | Where it fails | What you check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Text | First drafts, versions, fitting a length or reader | Added details, missed lengths, a generic voice | Every fact against your source, the length, the additions |
| Images | Illustrations, mockups, concept art | Garbled text, real-looking faces, wrong details | Text, faces, logos, accuracy, and your own alt text |
| Audio | Narration, translation, read-aloud access | Mispronounced names, cloned voices used without consent | Names, accuracy, and whose voice it is |
| Video | Short explainers, edits, captions | Fabricated scenes, people shown doing what they never did | Every scene against what happened, and every person's consent |

## 3.3 Human Versus AI Content

**AI-generated content** is any text, image, audio, or video that an AI tool produced, in whole or in part. People want to know whether something they read was AI-generated, and teachers, editors, and hiring managers want to know most of all. The honest answer is that you usually cannot tell by looking, and this section explains why and what works instead.

### What a Chat Assistant Says When You Ask

Here is a test. The passage below comes from *The Modern Bicycle and Its Accessories* (1898), a book now in the public domain. It was published long before any AI tool existed, so a person wrote it.

```text
Was the passage below written by AI or by a person? Answer AI or person, give your confidence as a percentage, and give two reasons.

Passage: Every one actively interested in the subject of tires will readily admit that the puncture proof tire is the tire of the future. Inventors are aiming to that end, and it is unfortunately true that the aim of most of them, while high, has not been straight or true. The result has been a confusion of so-called puncture proof tires, the non-utility and impracticability of which are observable at a glance. Steel, iron, wood, cork and chemical compounds have all been brought into use, and in such ludicrous shapes as to make the average puncture proof tire an object of deserved ridicule.
```

Sample response (captured from a chat assistant, October 2026). Your output will differ:

```text
**Answer: Person**

**Confidence: 90%**

**Reasons:**

1. **Period diction and style.** Phrases like "Every one actively interested," "unfortunately true that the aim... while high, has not been straight or true," and "the non-utility and impracticability of which are observable at a glance" read as early 20th-century trade-journal prose. The spelling "Every one" as two words, the heavy Latinate nouns ("non-utility"), and the dated subject (puncture-proof tires as "the tire of the future") point to an authentic historical source from the early automobile and bicycle era, not a modern imitation.
[...]
```

That is the right answer. Now send the same question about the bike clinic blurb from Section 3.2, which a chat assistant wrote. The passage was the blurb's three paragraphs joined into one, with the title removed:

Sample response (captured from a chat assistant, October 2026). Your output will differ:

```text
**Answer: AI**

**Confidence: 75%**

**Reasons:**

1. **Polished, generic closing and framing.** Lines like "Our neighborhood nonprofit believes everyone deserves a reliable ride" and "Stop by, say hello, and get back on the road with a bike that works the way it should" are smooth, upbeat marketing phrases. They feel templated and have no specific details, such as the organization's name, the church's name, or a real anecdote.
[...]
```

That is the right answer this time, and two more fresh runs agreed, at 70 percent each. Look at the reasons, though. The first quotes the two lines the tool itself added to the blurb. It also faults the passage for naming no organization or church, details the volunteer's prompt told the tool not to invent. A person writing under the same rule would have left them out too. Both answers rest on style, and style is a guess. The percentages sound measured, but nothing was measured: the same question about the same passage came back at 75, 70, and 70. A chat assistant asked "was this written by AI?" is predicting a likely-sounding verdict, the same way it predicts any other text.

### Why Detectors Fail

An **AI detector** is a tool that scores a piece of text on how likely it is to be AI-generated. Schools and employers have bought them, and the published evidence against them is strong:

* A Stanford team tested seven widely used detectors in 2023. The detectors were nearly perfect on essays by US eighth graders. They flagged more than half of 91 essays by non-native English writers as AI-generated, with an average false positive rate of about 61 percent.
* A team of European researchers tested 14 detection tools in 2023, including two commercial systems. All 14 scored below 80 percent accuracy, and the authors concluded the tools were "neither accurate nor reliable."

Both studies appear in Further Reading with their dates. A false positive here is not a small error. It is a person accused of something they did not do, and the Stanford study shows the accusations land hardest on people writing in a second language.

### What Works Instead

Two kinds of information answer the question better than any detector.

The first is evidence of process. A person who wrote something can show drafts, notes, version history, and sources, and a person who used AI can show the prompt log. Your log from Chapter 1 is exactly this kind of evidence.

The second is **disclosure**: a plain statement of whether and how AI helped make something. Disclosure moves the question from "can we catch it?" to "did they tell us?" A reader can judge disclosed work fairly, while a detector score gives them nothing to evaluate.

As of October 2026, some tools attach **content credentials**, a record embedded in a file that says how it was made and edited, including whether an AI tool produced it. The Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA), a standards group, publishes the open standard behind them. Credentials help when they are present. They can be stripped when a file is re-saved, uploaded to some sites, or screenshotted, so a missing credential proves nothing.

## 3.4 Ethical Use Principles

You have checked leads and shaped a draft. You have also seen why detectors fail. This section gives you the principles that tie those habits together and the two tools you will hand to anyone who asks how you used AI. Chapter 1 named the role: the **human in the loop**, the person who directs an AI tool, checks its work, and stays responsible for what goes out under their name.

### Five Principles

Each principle comes with the question you ask before you send or publish:

* **Honesty** (presenting the work as what it is). Does my reader know AI helped?
* **Attribution** (giving credit to the people and sources whose work you used). Did I credit every source, and did I check that each one says what I claim?
* **Consent** (a person's clear permission to use their words, image, voice, or story). Did everyone whose information or likeness appears agree to this use?
* **Privacy** (keeping other people's information, and your own, out of places it does not belong). Did anything go into the tool that I would not post in public?
* **Harm** (the damage work can do when it is wrong or misused). Could this mislead, embarrass, or hurt someone, and who carries that risk?

The principles sometimes conflict, since a story told with consent can still harm someone if it identifies another person who did not agree. When two pull against each other, write down both and the choice you made. That record is what lets you defend the choice.

### The Disclosure Statement

A disclosure must say three things: what the AI did, what you did, and what you checked. Here is a food truck owner's first try at getting one:

```text
I run a food truck. I used a chat assistant to brainstorm names for a new breakfast taco and to write a first draft of its menu description. I picked the name myself, rewrote the description, and checked every ingredient against my recipe. Write a disclosure statement about my AI use for the menu page of my website.
```

Sample response (captured from a chat assistant, October 2026). Your output will differ:

```text
**A note on how we use AI**

When we developed our newest breakfast taco, we used an AI chat assistant to help brainstorm possible names and to write a first draft of the menu description. That's where its role ended.

The name was chosen by the owner. The description was rewritten in our own words, and every ingredient listed was checked against our actual recipe. What you read here, and what ends up in your taco, is what we make by hand in our kitchen.

Questions about ingredients or allergens? Ask us at the truck or get in touch through our contact page. We're always happy to talk tacos.
[...]
**Shorter version, if you'd like one for a footer or menu card:**

*Our new breakfast taco's name and description were developed with help from an AI brainstorming tool, then chosen, rewritten, and fact-checked by us against our recipe.*
```

The tool offered two versions: a full note and, at the end, a shorter one. The short version meets the three-part rule and adds nothing. The full note adds claims the owner never made. It says everything is "what we make by hand in our kitchen," and it sends customers to a contact page she never mentioned. The tool flagged neither addition, so the owner has to read every line and choose. A tighter prompt removes the choice. Here is the tightened request, sent fresh:

```text
I run a food truck. I used a chat assistant to brainstorm names for a new breakfast taco and to write a first draft of its menu description. I picked the name myself, rewrote the description, and checked every ingredient against my recipe. Write a disclosure statement about my AI use for the menu page of my website. Use two sentences and under 35 words. Say what the AI did, what I did, and what I checked. Do not claim anything I did not tell you.
```

Sample response (captured from a chat assistant, October 2026). Your output will differ:

```text
I used an AI chat assistant to brainstorm names for this taco and draft its first menu description. I chose the name, rewrote the description myself, and checked every ingredient against my recipe.
```

Two sentences, 33 words by a word counter, and every claim matches what the owner said. The first sentence says what the AI did. The second says what she did and what she checked.

### The Privacy Check

A **privacy check** is a short list of what goes into an AI tool and what stays out, made before you send the prompt. A clinic's front desk asked a chat assistant for help with one:

```text
I work the front desk at a family clinic. Before I paste the draft below into a chat assistant to make it friendlier, list everything in it that I should remove or replace to protect privacy. One line per item. Do not rewrite the draft.

Draft: Hi Marisol, this is the front desk. Dr. Brennan wants to move your follow-up for your blood pressure check to Thursday at 3:15. Please call us back at 602-555-0142 or reply to this text. Your date of birth on file is 4/9/1971, so let us know if that is wrong.
```

Sample response (captured from a chat assistant, October 2026). Your output will differ:

```text
- **"Marisol"** (first name): identifies the patient. Replace with a placeholder like [Patient].
- **"Dr. Brennan"**: names the treating provider. Replace with [Provider] or "your doctor."
- **"follow-up for your blood pressure check"**: reveals a health condition or reason for care. Replace with a generic phrase like "your follow-up appointment."
- **"Thursday at 3:15"**: specific appointment date and time. Replace with [day/time].
- **"602-555-0142"**: the clinic's callback number, which is not patient data but is a specific phone number. Replace with [clinic phone number] if you'd rather not share it.
- **"4/9/1971"**: the patient's date of birth, a direct identifier. Remove it entirely or replace with [DOB].
- **"your date of birth on file"** (the sentence as a whole): asks for verification of identity details over text. Remove or replace with a generic request to call to confirm details.
- **"the front desk" / the clinic context** (family clinic): the clinic's name isn't in the draft, so nothing to remove, but don't add it when pasting.
```

The list is good, but the method is not. To get it, the front desk pasted the patient's name, condition, and birth date into the same tool the list was meant to protect. The check came after the leak. (The names and numbers in this example are fictional.) Even the list's last line, "don't add it when pasting," assumes the paste is going to happen.

Run the privacy check yourself, before any paste. Write two columns:

```text
WENT INTO THE TOOL            STAYED OUT
the task: make it friendlier  the patient's name
[Patient]                     the provider's name
[Provider]                    the condition
[Day/Time]                    the appointment time
[Clinic phone]                the date of birth
                              the phone number
```

The check takes two minutes. When a manager asks what you shared, this list is your answer.

### Who Owns What You Make

**Content ownership** is the question of who holds the rights to a piece of work, including work made with AI. Your own additions, such as the words you rewrote and the way you selected and arranged material, can still be yours, decided case by case. ## 3.5 Summary and Retrieval 💡

### Key Concepts

* A chat assistant is a research partner, not a final source. What it gives you is a lead, and you turn a lead into a fact by opening the primary source and recording a citation. A research tool's citations need the same opening, because a real page may not support the sentence it is attached to.
* Summaries of text you supply are the safest research job. Ask what the source does not say, and name any caveat that must survive, because a model decides on its own what to drop.
* Creative tools help with text, images, audio, and video, and each fails in a predictable way. Supply the facts, set the limits, and check the result against both. Count words yourself.
* You cannot reliably tell AI-made content from human-made by style, and neither can a chat assistant or a detector. Published 2023 studies found detectors inaccurate and biased against non-native English writers. Use evidence of process and disclosure instead.
* Five principles guide every use: honesty, attribution, consent, privacy, and harm. A disclosure says what the AI did, what you did, and what you checked, and adds nothing else.
* A privacy check lists what goes into a tool and what stays out, and you make it before the paste, never after.
* Under the US Copyright Office's 2025 guidance, prompts alone do not make you the author of AI output, though your own additions can be yours.

## Further Reading 📖

* [GPT Detectors Are Biased Against Non-Native English Writers (Liang et al., 2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02819): The Stanford study behind the detector findings in Section 3.3, later published in the journal Patterns. Read the abstract and Figure 1.
* [Testing of Detection Tools for AI-Generated Text (Weber-Wulff et al., 2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15666): A test of 14 detection tools, published in the International Journal for Educational Integrity. It reports that every tool scored below 80 percent accuracy and explains why the authors call the tools neither accurate nor reliable.
* [Copyright and Artificial Intelligence (U.S. Copyright Office)](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/): The Office's multi-part report. Part 2, published January 29, 2025, covers whether AI-generated output can be copyrighted. Chapter 8 returns to it.
* [Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA)](https://c2pa.org/): The standards group behind content credentials. Its pages explain how a file can carry a record of how it was made.
* [Guidance for Generative AI in Education and Research (UNESCO, 2023)](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research): A United Nations agency's guidance on using generative AI in schools and research, with a focus on human-centered use and data privacy.
